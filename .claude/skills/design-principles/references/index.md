# Universal Principles of Design — Index

Indexes the 125 principles of *Universal Principles of Design* (Lidwell, Holden, Butler; Revised & Updated). Tags: P = influence perception · L = help people learn · U = enhance usability · A = increase appeal · D = make better decisions. `p.N` is the printed page; the full text lives at PDF page N−2 in `~/code-projects/Resources/UniversalPrinciplesOfDesign.pdf`.

## Principles

### 80/20 Rule · p.14 · [U,D]
Roughly 80 percent of a system's effects come from 20 percent of its variables — 80 percent of usage from 20 percent of features, 80 percent of errors from 20 percent of components. Focusing resources on the critical 20 percent yields far more improvement than spreading effort evenly.
**Use when:** deciding which features or areas deserve redesign/testing budget and which can be cut or ignored.

### Accessibility · p.16 · [L,U,D]
Designs should be usable by people of diverse abilities without special modification, achieved through four qualities: perceptibility, operability, simplicity, and forgiveness. Accommodations built for disability usually end up benefiting everyone.
**Use when:** auditing whether a design excludes people with sensory, physical, cognitive, or literacy differences.

### Advance Organizer · p.18 · [L]
Presenting a brief, abstract "big picture" before detailed material helps people slot new information into what they already know, improving comprehension. Expository organizers work for unfamiliar topics; comparative organizers work when the audience has related knowledge.
**Use when:** introducing a linear lesson, onboarding flow, or presentation where the audience needs orientation before details.

### Aesthetic-Usability Effect · p.20 · [U,A]
People perceive more-aesthetic designs as easier to use than less-aesthetic ones, whether or not that's actually true — first impressions bias all subsequent interactions and make people more tolerant of flaws. Positive design relationships also unlock more creative, patient problem-solving.
**Use when:** justifying visual polish on a functional product, or explaining why a "plain but usable" design underperforms a more attractive rival.

### Affordance · p.22 · [P,U]
The physical characteristics of an object suggest its function — round wheels afford rolling, a flat plate affords pushing. When affordance matches intended use, the design performs better and needs no instructions; when it conflicts, people struggle (e.g., a pull-handle on a push door).
**Use when:** a control or object is being used incorrectly and you suspect its shape/appearance is signaling the wrong action.

### Alignment · p.24 · [P,A]
Aligning elements along shared edges, rows, columns, or a centerline creates unity, perceived stability, and a path for the eye to follow. Left/right-aligned text gives stronger alignment cues than centered text.
**Use when:** a layout feels disorganized or unstable and elements aren't sharing clear edges or axes.

### Anthropomorphic Form · p.26 · [P,A]
Forms that suggest human or humanlike features (faces, body proportions) attract attention and build emotional rapport, even in abstract or non-realistic renderings. Rounder forms read as babylike/friendly; angular forms read as masculine/aggressive.
**Use when:** a product or brand needs to feel approachable, trustworthy, or emotionally engaging rather than purely functional.

### Archetypes · p.28 · [A]
Universal patterns of theme and character (hero, outlaw, mentor) are hardwired cultural biases that, when aligned with a design's form, name, and story, increase its probability of success. Misaligned archetypes (e.g., a rebel brand using a hero pose) undercut the intended appeal.
**Use when:** defining brand identity, story, or character design and needing it to resonate on an instinctive, pre-verbal level.

### Area Alignment · p.30 · [A]
When elements are irregular or asymmetrical, aligning them by visual weight/area (not by bounding-box edges) produces the strongest perceived alignment, even though it creates a ragged edge. Standard software edge-alignment is wrong for pull quotes, bullets, and other non-uniform shapes.
**Use when:** edge-aligning dissimilar or irregularly shaped elements (pull quotes, bullets, mixed icon sizes) still looks visually off.

### Attractiveness Bias · p.32 · [A]
Attractive people are unconsciously judged as more intelligent, competent, and moral — a bias rooted in symmetry and waist-to-hip ratio as health/fertility signals. It shapes hiring, voting, and judicial outcomes, not just romantic preference.
**Use when:** choosing or rendering people in marketing/advertising imagery and needing to maximize positive audience response.

### Baby-Face Bias · p.34 · [A]
Round features, big eyes, and high foreheads read as naive, helpless, and honest — a bias that applies to characters, products, and adults alike. Baby-faced adults are trusted for testimonials but not for authority or expertise.
**Use when:** designing a character, mascot, or product form and choosing between "innocent/approachable" versus "authoritative/expert" positioning.

### Biophilia Effect · p.36 · [L]
Exposure to nature views or imagery (even photos, not real plants) reduces stress and measurably improves attention and concentration. The effect shows up in test scores, focus duration, and self-reported calm.
**Use when:** designing spaces or interfaces meant for learning, healing, or sustained concentration.

### Cathedral Effect · p.38 · [L]
Noticeably high ceilings prime abstract, creative thinking; noticeably low ceilings prime concrete, detail-oriented thinking — an effect driven by priming, not just comfort. The effect vanishes if ceiling height isn't consciously noticed.
**Use when:** planning a physical space (or its metaphorical equivalent) for either brainstorming/ideation or focused, detail-heavy work.

### Chunking · p.40 · [L]
Breaking information into small groups (the classic limit is 4±1 items) lets short-term memory hold and recall it far better than one long unbroken sequence. It applies specifically to tasks requiring memory/recall — not to reference material meant for scanning.
**Use when:** someone must remember or retain a list, sequence, or set of steps (not when they're just scanning/searching a reference).

### Classical Conditioning · p.42 · [L,A]
Repeatedly pairing a neutral stimulus (a product, brand) with something that triggers an automatic emotional response conditions that same response onto the stimulus over time. Positive pairings (attractive imagery) build appeal; negative pairings can create lasting aversion.
**Use when:** building brand or product affinity through repeated association with pleasant imagery, sound, or feeling.

### Closure · p.44 · [P]
People automatically perceive a set of fragmented or incomplete elements as one recognizable whole, filling in gaps subconsciously — the tendency is strongest when the pattern is simple and elements are near each other. This lets designers remove lines/detail and still read as complete (and even more engaging).
**Use when:** simplifying a logo, icon, or illustration and wanting to reduce elements while keeping it instantly recognizable.

### Cognitive Dissonance · p.46 · [A]
People feel discomfort when their attitudes, thoughts, or beliefs conflict, and resolve it by minimizing, justifying, or changing one of the conflicting cognitions. Small incentives change beliefs; large incentives just get rationalized as payment, leaving beliefs untouched.
**Use when:** an advertising or persuasion campaign needs to shift beliefs or values, not just secure a one-time behavior.

### Color · p.48 · [P,A]
Color attracts attention, groups elements, signals meaning, and adds aesthetic appeal, but should be used conservatively (roughly five colors max) since it carries no universal symbolic meaning across cultures. Saturation should track priority: saturated colors for attention, desaturated for sustained performance.
**Use when:** choosing a palette, or deciding whether color choices are doing real communicative work versus just decorating.

### Common Fate · p.50 · [P]
Elements that move together (same direction, timing, speed) or flicker in sync are perceived as one related group, even overriding grouping-by-similarity. Motion relative to a bounding region also determines whether elements read as figure or ground.
**Use when:** designing animated or moving UI elements and needing viewers to instantly perceive which pieces belong together.

### Comparison · p.52 · [L,D]
Relationships and patterns become legible only when data is presented "apples to apples" (common units), in a single context (so subtle differences are visible), and against a benchmark for scale. Without these, comparisons mislead or fail to register.
**Use when:** building a chart, report, or pitch that claims one thing is better/worse/bigger than another.

### Confirmation · p.54 · [U]
Requiring explicit verification before a critical or irreversible action prevents "slips" — unintended actions — but should be reserved for high-stakes operations since overuse causes people to ignore or auto-dismiss confirmations. Dialogs suit software; two-step arm/fire sequences suit hardware.
**Use when:** an action is destructive, irreversible, or costly to undo and users might trigger it by accident.

### Consistency · p.56 · [P,U]
Similar parts of a system should look and behave in similar ways — aesthetically, functionally, internally, and externally — so people can transfer learning from one part to another instead of relearning each time. Internal consistency also signals that a system was actually designed, not cobbled together.
**Use when:** a set of screens, controls, or components look/behave differently for no functional reason, forcing users to relearn each one.

### Constancy · p.58 · [P]
People perceive an object's size, brightness, shape, and loudness as stable even as sensory input changes with distance, lighting, or angle — perception reconciles input against memory, not raw signal. This matters for realistic renderings, where cues like distance and lighting need to behave the way constancy expects.
**Use when:** building simulations, renderings, or environments where inconsistent lighting/perspective cues would break believability.

### Constraint · p.60 · [U]
Limiting the possible actions on a system — physically (paths, axes, barriers) or psychologically (symbols, conventions, mappings) — reduces errors and makes correct use nearly automatic. Physical constraints stop bad input; psychological constraints make good input obvious.
**Use when:** users keep making the same wrong input or action and you want to make the error physically or perceptually impossible rather than just warning against it.

### Contour Bias · p.62 · [P]
Rounded, contoured objects are innately preferred over sharp/angular ones because pointed features activate the brain's fear-processing amygdala. Angular objects grab attention and provoke more thought, but rounded ones make a better first emotional impression.
**Use when:** choosing between rounded and angular form language for something emotionally neutral, and deciding whether to prioritize warmth or attention-grabbing edge.

### Control · p.64 · [U]
The amount of control offered should scale with a user's proficiency — beginners need structure and constrained choices, experts need direct, efficient access. Systems used often enough to build expertise should offer both a simple path and a fast/expert path (limit to two methods).
**Use when:** deciding whether to add a shortcut, power-user mode, or customization option versus keeping the interface simple.

### Convergence · p.66 · [D]
Systems that best fit their environment succeed and stabilize into similar forms over time (cars all converging on four wheels + steering wheel); high convergence signals a stable environment where only incremental innovation succeeds. Low convergence (an unstable, still-forming environment) rewards radical experimentation instead.
**Use when:** deciding whether to refine an established category convention or attempt a radical departure from it.

### Cost-Benefit · p.68 · [U,D]
A design is good when the benefits of interacting with it outweigh the costs (time, effort, money) — this determines usability tolerances like acceptable load time, not fixed universal thresholds. Designers often misjudge which features feel like costs versus benefits without observing real use.
**Use when:** deciding how much friction (steps, wait time, reading) a design can tolerate given the value it delivers.

### Defensible Space · p.70 · [A]
Spaces that show territorial markers, opportunities for surveillance, and signs of active care and ownership deter crime and feel safer. Territoriality, surveillance, and symbolic barriers (like tended plants) work together to signal "this space is watched and owned."
**Use when:** designing a residential, community, or public space and crime deterrence or perceived safety is a goal.

### Depth of Processing · p.72 · [L]
Information analyzed deeply (elaborative rehearsal — thinking about meaning) is remembered far better than information merely repeated (maintenance rehearsal). Distinctiveness, relevance, and required elaboration all increase how deeply — and memorably — information gets processed.
**Use when:** designing instructional content where you need long-term retention, not just momentary comprehension.

### Design by Committee · p.74 · [D]
Group-based, iterative design generally outperforms a single "visionary" dictator on quality, error-correction, and stakeholder buy-in, despite being slower — the myth of the lone genius designer is largely false. Dictatorship suits fast, low-stakes, simple projects; committee suits complex, high-stakes, quality-critical ones.
**Use when:** deciding whether a design decision needs broad review/consensus or can be made unilaterally to hit a deadline.

### Desire Line · p.76 · [U]
Worn paths, repeated errors, or other traces of real use reveal how people actually use a design, regardless of how it was intended to be used. Fighting a strong desire line with barriers usually fails; incorporating it into the redesign usually wins.
**Use when:** users consistently do something "wrong" or take an unintended shortcut and you're deciding whether to block it or redesign around it.

### Development Cycle · p.78 · [D]
Products move through four sequential stages — requirements, design, development, testing — each with distinct goals, and skipping or rushing a stage (especially requirements-gathering from real users) undermines everything after it. Requirements should come from controlled interaction with the audience, not from asking what they want.
**Use when:** planning or diagnosing a broken product process and figuring out which stage was shortchanged.

### Entry Point · p.80 · [U,A]
First impressions at a design's point of entry (a homepage, lobby, store front) disproportionately shape all later perception, so entry points need minimal barriers, a clear view of options (points of prospect), and lures that pull people further in.
**Use when:** a landing page, storefront, or lobby is losing people before they engage with the actual content.

### Errors · p.82 · [U,D]
Most "human error" is really design error; errors split into slips (unintended actions, from interruption/habit) and mistakes (wrong intentions, from stress/bad decisions), each requiring different fixes — feedback and constraints for slips, visibility and reduced noise for mistakes.
**Use when:** diagnosing why users keep making a specific error, to determine whether it's a slip or a mistake and fix the right thing.

### Expectation Effect · p.84 · [D]
Expectations — a person's own, or those communicated by others — actively change perception and behavior (halo effect, placebo effect, Pygmalion effect), which means test results and reviews can be silently biased by what people were led to expect.
**Use when:** running user tests or evaluations and needing to rule out the possibility that expectations, not the design, produced the result.

### Exposure Effect · p.86 · [L,A]
Repeated exposure to a neutral or positive stimulus increases how much it's liked over time — familiarity itself builds appeal, which is why radically new designs (the Eiffel Tower, Guggenheim) face initial resistance that fades. The effect is strongest for the first ~10 exposures and weakens with longer, more obvious exposure.
**Use when:** launching something unconventional and needing to justify or plan for initial resistance that should fade with repeated contact.

### Face-ism Ratio · p.88 · [P,A]
The ratio of face to body shown in an image shifts perception: high face-ism (mostly face) reads as intelligent/dominant/ambitious, low face-ism (mostly body) reads as physical/sensual — independent of the subject's or viewer's gender.
**Use when:** cropping or framing a photo of a person and deciding whether to emphasize competence/authority versus physicality.

### Factor of Safety · p.90 · [D]
Building in more capacity than believed necessary offsets unknown variables and prevents failure; the size of the safety margin should scale with how much is unknown about the design's conditions and materials. Margins tend to get trimmed over time for cost — until a failure forces them back up.
**Use when:** setting capacity, load limits, or tolerances for something new where key variables are still uncertain.

### Feedback Loop · p.92 · [D]
Every design change ripples back through the system as new input — positive loops amplify change (and can spiral out of control), negative loops dampen and stabilize it. Fixing one variable in isolation, without accounting for how people's behavior adapts, can make the overall problem worse (safer football helmets → riskier tackling).
**Use when:** a fix to one part of a system seems to be causing an unexpected new problem elsewhere.

### Fibonacci Sequence · p.94 · [A]
A numeric sequence where each number is the sum of the prior two, found throughout natural forms (shells, flowers) and closely tied to the golden ratio; patterns based on it are widely felt to be intrinsically harmonious. It should emerge from natural exploration, not be forced onto a composition.
**Use when:** developing an organic, rhythmic composition or geometric pattern and looking for a proportion system that doesn't feel arbitrary.

### Figure-Ground Relationship · p.96 · [P]
Perception splits any scene into a "figure" (object of focus) and "ground" (undifferentiated background); clear figure-ground cues (defined shape, closer position, lower placement) make the figure get more attention and better recall. Ambiguous figure-ground relationships flip back and forth and create visual confusion.
**Use when:** an important element isn't standing out from its background, or a design feels visually ambiguous about what to look at.

### Fitts' Law · p.98 · [U]
The time to move to and hit a target is a function of its size and distance — smaller/farther targets take longer and produce more errors, especially under speed pressure. Constraining movement (screen edges, corners) or enlarging/nearing frequent targets improves speed and accuracy.
**Use when:** sizing or placing a button, control, or click target that people need to hit quickly and accurately.

### Five Hat Racks · p.100 · [P]
There are only five ways to organize any body of information: category, time, location, alphabet, or continuum — choosing the right one (or combination) depends on how people will naturally look for the content. Alphabet is the fallback when no other organizing principle clearly applies.
**Use when:** structuring a menu, index, or content hierarchy and unsure what organizing scheme will make items findable.

### Flexibility-Usability Tradeoff · p.102 · [D]
As a design becomes more flexible (does more things) it necessarily becomes less usable for any single task — a Swiss Army knife beats no single tool at being a knife. Favor flexibility when the audience's future needs are unclear; favor specialization when needs are well understood.
**Use when:** deciding whether to build one flexible/general tool or several specialized ones for a set of known tasks.

### Forgiveness · p.104 · [L,U]
Good designs prevent errors and soften their consequences when they happen, primarily through affordances, reversibility (undo), and safety nets — with confirmations/warnings/help as weaker backups. The amount of help text a design needs is inversely proportional to how well it's actually designed.
**Use when:** deciding how to handle the aftermath of user error — undo, safety net, warning, or just better upstream affordances.

### Form Follows Function · p.106 · [D]
Beauty descriptively results from a design serving its function with no unnecessary ornament — but "function must dictate form" as a strict rule is a myth (function doesn't actually follow form in nature either). The real question isn't form-vs-function, it's which aspects are truly critical to success.
**Use when:** a team is deadlocked over whether to sacrifice an aesthetic or functional element, and needs a framework beyond "form vs. function."

### Framing · p.108 · [A]
The same information produces different judgments depending on whether it's presented positively or negatively (95% fat-free vs. 5% fat) — framing is neutralized when people see multiple conflicting frames at once. Positive frames push people to act; negative frames push people to avoid acting.
**Use when:** writing persuasive copy and deciding whether to emphasize the upside or the downside to drive the desired behavior.

### Freeze-Flight-Fight-Forfeit · p.110 · [U,D]
Under acute stress, humans move through an ordered sequence of responses — freeze (hyperawareness), flight (escape), fight (aggression), forfeit (shutdown) — and design for high-stress contexts must anticipate degraded fine-motor and cognitive performance at each stage.
**Use when:** designing controls, signage, or procedures for emergencies or high-stress situations (evacuation, crisis response, safety systems).

### Garbage In–Garbage Out · p.112 · [L,D]
Output quality depends entirely on input quality; bad inputs come from either wrong-type errors (mistakes, fixed by affordances/constraints) or wrong-quality errors (slips, fixed by previews/confirmations). Preventing garbage input is always cheaper than correcting garbage output.
**Use when:** a form, database, or process keeps producing bad output and the fix needs to happen at data entry, not after the fact.

### Golden Ratio · p.114 · [A]
A proportion (~0.618) found throughout nature and classical art/architecture that's widely felt to be intrinsically pleasing, though whether that preference is innate or culturally learned is still debated. It should be explored opportunistically, never forced at the expense of other design goals.
**Use when:** setting proportions for a layout, logo, or composition and wanting a time-tested ratio as a starting reference, not a rigid constraint.

### Good Continuation · p.116 · [P]
Elements arranged along a straight line or smooth curve are perceived as a related group, and the eye continues along an established direction rather than perceiving an abrupt branch or bend. Aligning related elements along a shared path (and unrelated ones off it) signals relatedness even across gaps.
**Use when:** arranging data points, icons, or elements where you want viewers to instinctively read a sequence or relatedness from their path.

### Gutenberg Diagram · p.118 · [P]
For evenly distributed, homogeneous, text-heavy layouts, Western readers' eyes sweep from a primary optical area (top-left) to a terminal area (bottom-right), leaving "fallow" zones (top-right, bottom-left) under-attended unless emphasized. It only predicts eye movement when nothing else (weight, imagery) is directing attention instead.
**Use when:** laying out a text-heavy, evenly weighted page or ad and deciding where the headline, image, and call-to-action should sit.

### Hick's Law · p.120 · [U]
The time to decide among options increases with the number of options — but only for simple, single-response decisions, not complex tasks involving reading or problem-solving. Reducing the number of choices at a decision point speeds response time and cuts errors.
**Use when:** a simple, time-critical decision point (menu, button set, alarm response) has too many options and response time or errors are suffering.

### Hierarchy · p.122 · [L,U]
Making superordinate/subordinate relationships visually explicit — via trees, nests, or stairs — is the simplest way to help people understand a complex system's structure. Trees suit moderate complexity, nests suit simple groupings, stairs suit large/growing hierarchies.
**Use when:** a menu, org chart, or information structure feels flat or confusing and users can't tell what belongs under what.

### Hierarchy of Needs · p.124 · [D]
A design must satisfy needs in order — functionality, reliability, usability, proficiency, then creativity — before higher-level needs can matter; polishing creativity/delight while functionality is still broken is wasted effort. Each level unlocks the next.
**Use when:** deciding what to fix first on a product that has problems at multiple levels (broken core function vs. missing delight features).

### Highlighting · p.126 · [P]
Bold, color, inversing, and similar techniques draw attention to specific elements, but only work if used sparingly (under ~10% of a display) and consistently — overuse dilutes the effect until nothing stands out. Bolding is generally the safest, cleanest technique; underlining and blinking carry the most noise/legibility cost.
**Use when:** deciding how to emphasize one element among many without visually overwhelming the rest of the design.

### Horror Vacui · p.128 · [P]
A tendency (and aesthetic style) of filling every empty space with content — the opposite of minimalism. Research shows filled, cluttered displays are perceived as lower value/prestige than sparse ones; minimalism signals affluence and quality to educated/affluent audiences.
**Use when:** deciding how much white space to leave in a design meant to signal premium quality versus abundant value.

### Hunter-Nurturer Fixations · p.130 · [A]
Male children show innate preference for object-movement, weapons, and physical/predator play; female children show innate preference for facial expression, nurturing, and verbal play — a biologically rooted, not purely cultural, gender difference in early play preference.
**Use when:** designing toys, characters, or play experiences targeted specifically at young boys or young girls.

### Iconic Representation · p.132 · [P,U]
Pictorial icons speed recognition and recall, and come in four types — similar (looks like the thing), example (a representative instance), symbolic (established abstraction), and arbitrary (must be learned) — matched to how concrete or standardized the concept is.
**Use when:** choosing or designing an icon and deciding how literal versus abstract it should be based on audience familiarity.

### Immersion · p.134 · [L,U]
A state of intense focus and joy occurs when perceptual/cognitive systems are challenged near capacity without being overwhelmed — requiring clear goals, minimal distraction, immediate feedback, and a sense of control. Games achieve it through sensory richness; complex tasks like chess achieve it through cognitive engagement alone.
**Use when:** designing an experience (game, exhibit, task flow) meant to fully absorb attention and produce flow.

### Inattentional Blindness · p.136 · [P]
When focused on a task, people become effectively blind to unrelated stimuli directly in their field of view — even large or "obvious" ones — and counterintuitively, unexpected stimuli are noticed even less than anticipated ones. Faces, personal names, and threat stimuli are among the few things that reliably break through.
**Use when:** a warning, alert, or important element is being missed by task-focused users despite being clearly visible.

### Interference Effects · p.138 · [P,L,U]
Competing mental processes slow and degrade performance — Stroop/Garner interference from conflicting perceptual cues (a red "go" button), proactive/retroactive interference from old or new learning colliding with memory. Avoid conflicting coding (color vs. meaning) and space out learning with rest periods to minimize it.
**Use when:** a color, label, or icon's coding conflicts with its meaning, or new training seems to be corrupting previously learned material.

### Inverted Pyramid · p.140 · [L,U]
Presenting the most critical information first, then supporting detail in descending order of importance, lets people get the point fast, remember the lead best, and stop reading whenever they've gotten enough — at the cost of suspense or narrative build.
**Use when:** writing a report, article, or notification where readers may stop at any point and need the key takeaway first.

### Iteration · p.142 · [D]
Complex, well-tuned designs emerge only through repeated cycles of testing and refinement, not from a single pass; design iteration (expected, valuable) should be distinguished from development iteration (unplanned rework from bad specs, which is pure waste).
**Use when:** planning a design process and deciding how much testing/refinement time to build in versus treating the first draft as final.

### Law of Prägnanz · p.144 · [P]
When an image is ambiguous, people default to interpreting it in the simplest possible way — fewer elements, more symmetry — because that's what's easiest to encode and recall. Simpler compositions are easier to process; symmetrical ones read as more stable but less interesting than asymmetrical ones.
**Use when:** an ambiguous or complex composition is being misread, and simplifying its elements or symmetry would make the intended interpretation obvious.

### Layering · p.146 · [P,L,U]
Organizing information into related groupings and revealing only relevant layers at a time manages complexity — two-dimensional layering (linear, hierarchical, parallel, or web) shows one layer at a time; three-dimensional layering (opaque or transparent) shows multiple layers at once.
**Use when:** a display has too much information visible at once and needs a strategy for what to show by default versus on demand.

### Legibility · p.148 · [P,L]
Text clarity depends on size, typeface, contrast, line length, and spacing — concrete guidelines include 9–12pt body text, 70%+ contrast, and 35–55 characters per line for comfortable (though not fastest) reading.
**Use when:** setting or auditing typographic specs (size, contrast, line length, leading) for body text.

### Life Cycle · p.150 · [D]
Every product moves through introduction, growth, maturity, and decline, each demanding a different design focus — from tuning with early adopters, to scaling supply, to refining for retention, to managing transition to the next generation.
**Use when:** deciding what a product team should be prioritizing right now given where the product sits in its market lifecycle.

### Mapping · p.152 · [P,U]
A control's relationship to its effect is "good" or "natural" when the control's layout, behavior, or meaning corresponds to what it does (a window switch that moves the same direction as the window). Poor mapping — a control's motion doesn't match its effect — makes even simple actions confusing.
**Use when:** a control's direction or behavior doesn't intuitively match its effect and users keep guessing wrong.

### Mental Model · p.154 · [L,U]
People interact with systems based on internal representations built from experience; designers typically have accurate system models but weak interaction models, while users build the reverse. Good design merges an accurate system model with a validated interaction model, ideally by observing real use.
**Use when:** a design that "works correctly" from an engineering standpoint still confuses real users — the gap is between the system model and the interaction model.

### Mimicry · p.156 · [U,A]
Copying properties of familiar things — surface (looks like), behavioral (acts like), or functional (works like) — transfers existing understanding to a new design, improving usability or likeability. Functional mimicry is riskiest since physical principles don't always transfer across scale or context.
**Use when:** designing something new and unfamiliar and needing to borrow instant comprehension from an existing familiar object or behavior.

### Mnemonic Device · p.158 · [L]
Reorganizing information using vivid imagery or word tricks (first-letter acronyms, keywords, rhymes, feature-names) makes it dramatically easier to remember, especially names, rote facts, and procedures.
**Use when:** naming a product/campaign or designing instructional content where a sequence or list needs to be memorized easily.

### Modularity · p.160 · [D]
Breaking a large system into interdependent, self-contained modules with simple interfaces reduces complexity and improves reliability, flexibility, and upgradeability — at the cost of requiring deep upfront system knowledge to decompose it correctly. Most modular systems evolve into modularity incrementally rather than starting that way.
**Use when:** a system is becoming too complex to maintain or extend as one monolithic whole.

### Most Advanced Yet Acceptable · p.162 · [D]
The most commercially successful design is the most novel one that's still recognizable as something familiar — a balance point between typicality (comfort) and novelty (interest) known as MAYA. This applies to mass audiences; expert/art audiences weigh novelty more heavily and don't follow the same rule.
**Use when:** launching an innovative product to a mainstream audience and calibrating how far to push novelty without losing recognizability.

### Most Average Facial Appearance Effect · p.164 · [A]
Faces closer to their population's statistical average are perceived as more attractive — likely a mix of evolved fitness signaling, cognitive prototype matching, and symmetry. Composited/averaged faces of a population predict local perceptions of beauty.
**Use when:** creating or selecting a "generic attractive" face for advertising or a character and lacking a real model to use.

### Normal Distribution · p.166 · [D]
Many real-world variables plot as a symmetrical bell curve, but designing to the average (the "average person fallacy") badly under-serves most people, since being average on one measure doesn't mean being average on others. Design to accommodate roughly the 1st–99th percentile rather than a single average value.
**Use when:** setting a size, tolerance, or parameter (seat width, font size, load capacity) meant to fit a broad population.

### Not Invented Here · p.168 · [D]
Organizations resist ideas or solutions from outside sources (fear of losing control, desire for credit, sunk investment), leading to redundant, worse-than-necessary reinvention. Rotating team members, engaging outsiders, and monitoring competitors are the main preventive measures.
**Use when:** a team keeps rejecting proven outside solutions or insists on reinventing something that already exists elsewhere.

### Nudge · p.170 · [U]
Behavior can be predictably shifted without restricting choice or changing incentives, mainly through smart defaults, visible feedback, aligned incentives, structured choices, and visible goals. People overwhelmingly take the path of least resistance, so the default option matters enormously.
**Use when:** wanting to steer behavior toward a better outcome (enrollment, safety, savings) without removing choice or adding cost.

### Ockham's Razor · p.172 · [D]
Given two functionally equivalent designs, the simpler one should always be chosen — unnecessary elements add cognitive, visual, or physical weight and increase the chance of something going wrong. Apply it by evaluating and cutting every element that isn't required for function.
**Use when:** choosing between two designs that perform equally well and needing a tiebreaker principle.

### Operant Conditioning · p.174 · [L,A]
Behavior can be shaped through positive reinforcement (reward), negative reinforcement (removing an annoyance), or punishment — reinforcement should be favored over punishment, with predictable rewards early in training and unpredictable rewards later to build resistance to extinction.
**Use when:** designing a rewards, gamification, or habit-building system meant to reinforce specific user behaviors over time.

### Orientation Sensitivity · p.176 · [P]
Vertical and horizontal lines are perceived and judged more accurately than oblique ones (the oblique effect), and elements differing in orientation by 30°+ from their surroundings "pop out" and are quickly detected (the pop-out effect).
**Use when:** designing a display where people must quickly detect a line, texture, or element that differs in angle from its neighbors (dials, diagrams, target-spotting tasks).

### Performance Load · p.178 · [L,U]
The more mental (cognitive) or physical (kinematic) effort a task requires, the more likely it is to fail — reducible by chunking, memory aids, and automation (cognitive) or fewer steps and less motion (kinematic).
**Use when:** a task is taking too long or generating too many errors and you need to identify whether the burden is mental or physical.

### Performance Versus Preference · p.180 · [D]
What helps people perform best and what people say they prefer are often different things (the Dvorak keyboard is faster but unpopular) — surveys and stated preference are unreliable predictors of real use, especially for unfamiliar designs. Observing real behavior in context beats asking what people want.
**Use when:** user research shows people say they like design A but perform better with design B, and you must decide which to trust.

### Personas · p.182 · [U,D]
Creating a small number of fictional archetypal users, grounded in real interviews and data, focuses design decisions on serving the critical few well rather than the average visitor poorly. Keep to roughly three primary personas (plus up to four secondary) and role-play them to build design-team empathy.
**Use when:** a team is designing for a vague, generic "everyone" and decisions keep stalling without a concrete user to reference.

### Picture Superiority Effect · p.184 · [L]
Pictures are recalled better than words after more than ~30 seconds have passed, especially concrete/distinct images — though pictures plus supporting text beat either alone. The effect is strongest for casual, brief exposure and weakens as information complexity increases.
**Use when:** designing an ad, sign, or instructional material meant to be remembered later, not just understood in the moment.

### Priming · p.186 · [P]
Any sensory stimulus automatically activates related concepts in memory that then influence subsequent, seemingly unrelated behavior — most powerfully when the person doesn't consciously notice the connection (indirect priming beats obvious/direct priming).
**Use when:** setting up a first impression, adjacent context, or packaging cue meant to unconsciously shape how the main content is received.

### Progressive Disclosure · p.188 · [L,U]
Showing only necessary or requested information at any moment, and hiding the rest behind a simple "more" action, prevents overload, reduces errors, and speeds learning for novices and infrequent users, without hobbling advanced users who can dig deeper.
**Use when:** a screen or form feels overwhelming because of many rarely-used options and you need a default/advanced split.

### Propositional Density · p.190 · [A]
Designs that pack many layers of meaning (deep propositions) into a few simple visible elements (surface propositions) are more interesting and memorable — like puns, or the Apple logo's stacked associations. High density from few elements is more compelling than complexity for its own sake.
**Use when:** a logo, name, or simple visual element feels flat and needs more layered meaning without adding visual complexity.

### Prospect-Refuge · p.192 · [A]
People prefer environments offering both unobstructed views (prospect) and safe, concealed retreat (refuge) — spaces at the edges, with cover overhead and few access points, feel safest and are considered most aesthetic, especially in perceived-hazardous settings.
**Use when:** designing a landscape, seating layout, or room and deciding how to balance open sightlines against cozy, enclosed nooks.

### Prototyping · p.194 · [D]
Simplified, incomplete models — concept (quick exploration), throwaway (isolated functional testing), or evolutionary (continuously refined into the final product) — let designers learn and de-risk before committing to a final build, each with its own failure mode (artificial realism, scaling assumptions, tunnel vision).
**Use when:** deciding how to de-risk an uncertain design before full investment, and which type of prototype fits the uncertainty.

### Proximity · p.196 · [P]
Elements placed close together are perceived as more related than elements farther apart — one of the strongest and most reliable grouping cues, generally overpowering competing cues like similarity. Labels should sit directly next to what they describe rather than in a separate legend.
**Use when:** unrelated elements are visually reading as a group (or related elements aren't), simply due to their spacing.

### Readability · p.198 · [L,U]
How easily prose can be understood, driven by word/sentence length and complexity — complex content needs the *simplest* possible presentation, not a more elaborate one, so the reader's effort goes toward the ideas rather than parsing the sentences.
**Use when:** writing copy for a specific audience's reading level and needing to verify or simplify sentence/word complexity.

### Recognition Over Recall · p.200 · [L,U]
It's far easier to recognize something from a list of options than to recall it unprompted from memory — this is why menus beat command-line interfaces, and why a familiar brand often beats an objectively better unfamiliar one.
**Use when:** an interface forces users to remember commands, codes, or steps from memory instead of letting them pick from visible options.

### Red Effect · p.202 · [P,A]
Red clothing makes women appear more sexually attractive and men appear more dominant, mirroring red's role as a fertility/dominance signal across the animal kingdom — an effect confirmed even in competitive sports outcomes.
**Use when:** choosing an accent color for imagery of people meant to signal attractiveness (female) or dominance/authority (male).

### Redundancy · p.204 · [D]
Adding more elements than strictly necessary protects a system against failure of any single element — diverse redundancy (different element types) resists single-cause failure best; homogenous, active, and passive redundancy trade off simplicity against resilience.
**Use when:** a system's failure would be catastrophic and you need to decide what kind of backup/duplication to build in.

### Rosetta Stone · p.206 · [L]
Embedding an element of common understanding (a "key") that the audience already grasps lets you unlock the meaning of unfamiliar information — most effective when the key is unmistakably marked as a key and revealed in supporting stages.
**Use when:** explaining an unfamiliar concept to an audience and needing a bridge from something they already understand.

### Rule of Thirds · p.208 · [A]
Dividing a composition into thirds (a 3×3 grid) and placing the primary element on an intersection produces an asymmetrical, generally more interesting composition than dead-centering it — though centering can still work when the element is strong enough to anchor the whole composition on its own.
**Use when:** composing a photo, poster, or layout and deciding where the focal element should sit.

### Satisficing · p.210 · [D]
For very complex problems, time-limited problems, or problems where "better" yields diminishing returns, choosing the first satisfactory solution beats exhaustively searching for the optimal one. It shouldn't be used to justify a solution worse than what already exists.
**Use when:** a team is burning time chasing a "perfect" solution to a problem that's either too complex, too urgent, or too low-stakes to warrant it.

### Savanna Preference · p.212 · [A]
People innately prefer open, savanna-like landscapes — scattered trees, uniform grass, water, depth — over dense, arid, or overly complex terrain, an evolved preference strongest in young children and weakening with age/cultural influence.
**Use when:** designing a landscape, park, golf course, or nature-themed environment/imagery and wanting broadly appealing, comfortable scenery.

### Scaling Fallacy · p.214 · [D]
Assuming a design that works at one scale will work the same way at a larger or smaller scale is a common and costly error — both in physical load assumptions (stresses don't scale linearly) and in interaction assumptions (new scale creates new use cases, like suicide risk on tall buildings).
**Use when:** scaling up or down an existing successful design (product, building, system) and assuming its behavior/requirements will scale linearly too.

### Scarcity · p.216 · [A]
Items perceived as limited in supply, access, time, or number become more desirable — five tactics (exclusive info, limited access, limited time, limited number, sudden depletion) reliably move people to act, especially when scarcity is visible and competitive.
**Use when:** designing a promotion, launch, or offer meant to create urgency and drive immediate action.

### Self-Similarity · p.218 · [A]
Forms made of parts that resemble the whole (a coastline's edge pattern at any zoom level) are found throughout nature via recursion and read as intrinsically aesthetic — reusing one basic form across multiple levels of scale creates rich, cohesive complexity.
**Use when:** building a visual system, structure, or pattern that needs to feel organically cohesive across multiple levels of detail.

### Serial Position Effects · p.220 · [L]
Items at the beginning (primacy) and end (recency) of a list are recalled far better than items in the middle — primacy is durable (stored in long-term memory) while recency fades quickly once attention shifts elsewhere.
**Use when:** ordering items in a list, menu, or presentation and deciding where the most important item should go for maximum recall.

### Shaping · p.222 · [L]
Complex behaviors are taught by breaking them into small sub-behaviors and reinforcing each successive approximation until they chain into the full behavior — used (often invisibly) in game level design and sales tactics. Watch for incidental behaviors getting reinforced along with the intended one.
**Use when:** designing an onboarding flow, tutorial, or game progression meant to teach a complex skill step by step.

### Signal-to-Noise Ratio · p.224 · [P,L,U,A]
Design clarity is the ratio of relevant information (signal) to irrelevant clutter (noise) — maximized by presenting information simply and accurately, and minimized noise by removing every unnecessary element and toning down necessary ones (thin gridlines, minimal decoration).
**Use when:** a display or document feels cluttered or unclear and you need a general framework for deciding what to cut versus keep.

### Similarity · p.226 · [A]
Elements sharing color, size, or shape are perceived as more related than dissimilar elements — color grouping is strongest, size grouping works well when sizes are clearly distinguishable, and shape grouping is the weakest cue on its own.
**Use when:** wanting to visually group unlabeled items by category using only color, size, or shape, without adding lines or containers.

### Stickiness · p.228 · [L,A]
Ideas that are simple, surprising, concrete, credible, emotional, and story-based ("SUCCES") become far more memorable and more likely to be spontaneously shared — six levers that together make messaging spread on their own.
**Use when:** crafting a slogan, campaign message, or key idea that needs to be remembered and repeated by others without prompting.

### Storytelling · p.230 · [L,A]
Engaging an audience through setting, characters, plot, invisibility (forgetting the medium), mood, and movement makes information more emotionally resonant and personally recalled than flat presentation — a phenomenon essentially unique to narrative.
**Use when:** presenting information or a product experience that needs emotional resonance and personal recall, not just comprehension.

### Structural Forms · p.232 · [D]
Any load-bearing or containing structure is built from mass (solid, robust, simple), frame (light, flexible, triangulated), or shell (thin, efficient, but fragile to point loads) construction — each suited to different materials, scale, and risk tolerance.
**Use when:** choosing a physical or structural construction approach for something that must support a load or contain a volume.

### Symmetry · p.234 · [A]
Reflection, rotation, or translation symmetry signals balance and stability and is found throughout nature; symmetric forms are read as figure rather than ground, get more attention, and are recalled and judged as more attractive than asymmetric ones.
**Use when:** deciding whether a composition or face/character design should be symmetrical (for stability/recall) or asymmetrical (for interest).

### Threat Detection · p.236 · [P]
Evolutionarily threatening shapes (snake curves, spider legs, angry V-shaped eyebrows) are detected faster than neutral stimuli through a subconscious, parallel visual process — abstracted threat features can grab attention without the accompanying fear response.
**Use when:** needing an element to cut through a busy, noisy display and grab attention fast, without necessarily triggering alarm.

### Three-Dimensional Projection · p.238 · [P]
A handful of visual cues — interposition, size, elevation, linear perspective, texture gradient, shading, atmospheric perspective — trick flat images into reading as three-dimensional; combining more of these cues produces stronger depth illusion.
**Use when:** creating a flat (2D) image or icon that needs to read as having depth or dimensionality.

### Top-Down Lighting Bias · p.240 · [P,A,D]
People assume light comes from above (evolved from the sun), so shading at the top reads as concave and shading at the bottom reads as convex/natural — top-left lighting specifically is preferred and reads as most natural, especially for right-handed viewers.
**Use when:** rendering an icon, object, or scene and choosing a light source direction that will read as natural rather than unsettling.

### Uncanny Valley · p.242 · [A,D]
Anthropomorphic forms are appealing when clearly abstract or fully human, but become unsettling when very close to human without being identical — likely a subconscious pathogen-avoidance response, strongest with realistic fidelity paired with subtle flaws like unnatural eye movement.
**Use when:** designing a character, avatar, or robot with human-like features and deciding how far to push photorealism.

### Uncertainty Principle · p.244 · [D]
Measuring a sensitive variable can itself alter that variable and corrupt the measurement's accuracy — invasive measures (like asking people what they think) distort results more than unobtrusive ones (like observing real behavior).
**Use when:** designing a research method or metric and worried that the act of measuring will change the very behavior you're trying to observe.

### Uniform Connectedness · p.246 · [P]
Elements joined by a shared visual property — a bounding region or a connecting line — are perceived as more related than unconnected elements, and this cue is strong enough to override proximity or similarity, making it useful for fixing already-fixed layouts.
**Use when:** a layout's physical positions can't be changed but you still need to visually group certain controls or elements.

### Veblen Effect · p.248 · [A]
For visible, status-associated luxury goods, raising the price can increase (not decrease) demand, because price itself signals prestige — the effect requires the good to be visibly distinguishable from cheaper competitors and strongly tied to status.
**Use when:** pricing a luxury, prestige, or status-signaling product and deciding whether a lower price would actually hurt demand.

### Visibility · p.250 · [P,U]
A system is more usable when its status, available actions, and consequences are clearly visible — but "kitchen-sink visibility" (showing everything always) backfires by burying what matters; hierarchical organization and context-sensitivity solve this by revealing only what's relevant now.
**Use when:** users can't tell what state a system is in or what actions are currently possible, or a screen tries to show everything at once.

### Visuospatial Resonance · p.252 · [P]
An image can be tuned to read differently at different viewing distances by rendering content at different spatial frequencies (the Mona Lisa's smile, visible only in peripheral/distant vision) — high-frequency detail reads up close, low-frequency reads from afar.
**Use when:** creating an image or display meant to reveal different information or meaning depending on viewing distance.

### von Restorff Effect · p.254 · [L]
An item that stands out from its surrounding context (different in kind) or from past experience (different from memory) is recalled far better than typical items — but overusing the effect (highlighting everything) cancels it out entirely.
**Use when:** an item buried in the middle of a list or set needs to be more memorable, and you can afford to make just that one item different.

### Wabi-Sabi · p.256 · [A,D]
An aesthetic of naturalness, simplicity, and subtle imperfection (worn patina, handmade irregularity) creates a deeper, more meaningful beauty than flawless symmetry — distinct from mere sloppiness, since wabi-sabi objects still look cared-for and intentional.
**Use when:** a design feels sterile or over-polished and the brief calls for warmth, authenticity, or a handmade, imperfect quality.

### Waist-to-Hip Ratio · p.258 · [A,D]
A specific waist-to-hip ratio (~0.70 for women, ~0.90 for men) is perceived cross-culturally as most attractive, driven by its correlation with hormone levels and reproductive health signals — relevant whenever renderings or images of idealized human figures are needed.
**Use when:** designing or selecting renderings of idealized human figures for advertising or character work.

### Wayfinding · p.260 · [U]
Navigating any space (building, forest, website) follows four stages — orientation, route decision, route monitoring, destination recognition — each improved by distinct tactics: landmarks/signage, minimized choices, clear sightlines/breadcrumbs, and dead-end or barrier cues at the destination.
**Use when:** users are getting lost or disoriented moving through a physical space, site map, or multi-step flow.

### Weakest Link · p.262 · [D]
Deliberately designing in a weak element that fails first (a fuse, a sprinkler trigger) protects the rest of the system from a larger failure — this only works when a single failure condition can affect multiple connected elements.
**Use when:** protecting a system from catastrophic failure by designing in a deliberate, controlled point of failure.
